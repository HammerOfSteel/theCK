## THE CORNISH FOLK TRADITION

### What Makes Cornwall Different
Cornwall (*Kernow*) has a distinct cultural identity from the rest of England. It was the last stronghold of the Brythonic-Celtic language in England (Cornish/Kernewek, closely related to Welsh and Breton). Its folklore is unique, drawing on:
- **Celtic mythology** (shared with Wales, Brittany, Ireland)
- **Arthurian legend** (Tintagel, Lyonesse, the Cornish origin claims)
- **Mining folklore** (knockers, tommyknockers — underground spirits of the tin mines)
- **Maritime tradition** (mermaids, saints, fishermen's rituals)
- **Cunning folk** — the **pellar** tradition: herbalists, diviners, healers, charmers

Cornwall was never fully Anglicised in the way Devon and Somerset were. The last native Cornish speaker died in 1777 (Dolly Pentreath, from Mousehole), but the language has been revived and is now spoken by several thousand people. The *Cornish revival* is central to the culture Elena represents.

## THE PELLAR TRADITION

### Who Were the Pellars?
The *pellar* (from Cornish *pellor* — "one who repels" or "conjuror") was a folk healer and wise person found throughout Cornwall. Pellars were consulted for:
- **Healing** — herbal remedies, charm cures, laying on of hands
- **Divination** — scrying, reading signs, interpreting dreams
- **Protection** — against ill-wishing, the evil eye, curses
- **Finding lost things** — stolen cattle, missing objects, strayed animals
- **Breaking ill-luck** — counter-magic against perceived harmful magic

### Key Historical Pellars
| Name | Period | Location | Known For |
|------|--------|----------|-----------|
| **Tamsin Blight** | c.1798–1856 | Helston/Redruth area | Most famous Cornish pellar. Married a man named Blight who was also a cunning man. Known for curse-breaking and divination. |
| **James Thomas** | fl. 1830s–1850s | Illogan | "The Pellar of Helston" (often confused with Tamsin's husband). Used a crystal ball, herbal cures. |
| **Granny Boswell** | fl. 1880s–1910s | Penzance | Romani-Cornish healer. Used cards, herbs, and charms. Operated from a house near the harbour. |
| **The Pentreath Pellar** | oral tradition | Mousehole | Alleged pellar lineage associated with the last Cornish speakers. Unverified but persistent in local tradition. |

### Elena's Pellar Lineage
Elena Trevorran claims descent from a pellar tradition in the Penzance area. Her practices include:
- Herbal knowledge (real — based on documented Cornish herbalism)
- Divination through landscape reading (reading weather, animal behaviour, geological features)
- *Drolling* — telling traditional stories (*drolls*) which encode practical and spiritual knowledge
- Knowledge of sacred sites and their uses across seasons
- Counter-charm work (referenced obliquely — she doesn't advertise this)

**Design note:** Elena is NOT a fantasy witch. She is a culturally specific practitioner of a real folk tradition. Her practices are presented as a form of knowledge — parallel to psychology, not in opposition to it. Her central teaching: "Psychology studies the mind from the inside. The pellar tradition studies it through the land."

Elena doesn't teach Kernewek formally. She uses it and waits. If Amelia asks what a word means, Elena sometimes answers directly, sometimes with a droll. Examples:

> ELENA: *"An gwella."*
> AMELIA: "What does that mean?"
> ELENA: "If I could say it in English, I would. Some things only exist in the language they were born in."

> ELENA: *"Dydh da, Amelia. Fatla genes?"*
> AMELIA: "...Good day? How... are you?"
> ELENA: [slight smile] "You're learning."

### Detailed Site Descriptions

#### MÊN-AN-TOL — "The Stone with a Hole"
**Location:** Near Morvah, Penwith
**What it is:** A holed stone (about 1.3m diameter) flanked by two upright stones. Neolithic, purpose unknown.
**Folklore:**
- Children passed through the hole three times were cured of rickets
- Adults passed through nine times against the sun were cured of back pain
- The hole is a portal between worlds — "crawl through to be reborn"
- The pellar tradition used it for divination: look through the hole at certain times to see the future

**In the game (Maya path, Ch4):** Maya takes Amelia here. Maya is reverent; the place moves her. Amelia's response is a choice point:
- Participate (crawl through the hole) → OK/SD points
- Observe (take a photo, think about it) → SD points
- Resist (feel silly, don't engage) → no points

**Elena path variant:** Elena takes Amelia here at night. No crawling through. Just standing. Watching. Elena says: *"The stone has been here four thousand years. It doesn't care what you believe. Touch it."* Amelia does. Whether she feels anything is up to the player (and the Karma Dice).

#### THE MERRY MAIDENS — Stone Circle
**Location:** Near Lamorna, Penwith
**What it is:** A near-perfect circle of 19 granite stones, about 24m diameter. Bronze Age.
**Folklore:**
- Nineteen maidens turned to stone for dancing on a Sunday
- The two tall stones nearby (the Pipers) were the musicians who also turned to stone
- Local tradition: never count the stones. You'll always get a different number. (This is genuinely reported.)
- At certain times of year, the stones "dance" — tricks of light or genuine earth-mystery, depending on your tradition

**In the game (Elena path, Ch4):** Elena brings Amelia here and tells a *droll* — the story of the Maidens, but her version is older, sadder, and stranger than the tourist panel version. She asks: "What did the story tell you?" This is Elena's test.

**Hidden detail:** If the player has been tracking OK carefully, Amelia's journal entry for this scene includes: "I counted the stones. Nineteen. I think. I lost count and started over three times." (This is the actual reported phenomenon.)

---

#### MADRON HOLY WELL
**Location:** Near Madron, Penwith
**What it is:** A medieval well chapel with a spring that still flows. The well has been a site of healing and divination for centuries, possibly pre-Christian.
**Folklore:**
- Tie a strip of cloth (a *cloutie*) to the nearby tree and make a wish
- As the cloth rots, your ailment fades
- The well was used for divination: drop a pin or leaf onto the water and read the ripples
- Bishop Hall recorded miraculous healings here in the 17th century
- Still visited for healing. The *cloutie* tree is covered in strips of cloth.

**In the game (Maya/Elena path):** One of the optional Cornwall visits. The *cloutie* tree is described in detail — "strips of cloth in every colour, some new, some rotting, some just thread." Amelia can tie one. What she wishes for is a choice the game doesn't reveal — the journal simply says: "I tied a strip of my old t-shirt to the tree. I wished for something I'm not going to write down."

---

#### CARN EUNY — The Fogou
**Location:** Near Sancreed, Penwith
**What it is:** An Iron Age village with a *fogou* — an underground stone-lined chamber about 20m long. Purpose much debated: storage? ritual? refuge?
**Folklore:**
- The fogou was used for initiation rites (speculative but widely believed in folk tradition)
- It was a place to commune with chthonic spirits — the spirits of the earth
- In pellar tradition (per Elena), the fogou is a womb: "You go in one person and come out another"
- The temperature inside is constant year-round. The silence is described as "thick" or "alive"

**In the game (Elena path, Ch11):** THE climactic scene for the Alchemist ending. Amelia enters the fogou alone, by candlelight, at Elena's instruction. She stays for one hour. What happens in that hour is determined by the Karma Dice + OK + SD scores. But every version involves:
1. The candle light on ancient stone
2. Total silence (no ambient sound — this should be the only scene in the game with no audio)
3. Amelia's breathing becoming the loudest sound
4. A sense of something. What something depends on the player.

**Sensory detail (for writing):**
- The entrance is narrow — you have to duck
- The stone is cool to the touch, slightly damp
- The air smells of earth, stone, and something older
- Your eyes adjust slowly. The candle flame barely reaches the end.
- The silence isn't empty. It has *weight*.

---

#### ST. MICHAEL'S MOUNT
**Location:** Marazion, off the south coast of Penwith
**What it is:** Tidal island with a medieval castle and priory. Connected to the mainland by a causeway passable at low tide.
**Folklore:**
- Built by the giant Cormoran, slain by Jack (Jack the Giant Killer — Cornish origin)
- Part of the St. Michael Ley Line: St. Michael's Mount → Glastonbury → Avebury → and beyond
- The mount is said to be one of the *anchoring points* of spiritual energy in Britain
- At certain tides, the causeway appears and disappears — a natural metaphor for thresholds

**In the game:** Visible from several scenes but not visited directly (it's a tourist attraction and would feel too on-the-nose). Instead, it appears in the background of coastal scenes as a persistent visual symbol: *the thing you can see but can't quite reach. The castle on the water. The other place.*

**Elena reference:** "That mount has been a holy place since before the Normans, before the Saxons, before the Romans. Some places don't belong to anyone. They belong to themselves."

---

#### BODMIN MOOR
**Location:** Central-eastern Cornwall
**What it is:** A granite moorland with stone circles, stone rows, tors, and archaeological sites.
**Key sites:**
- **The Hurlers** — Three stone circles in a row. Legend: men turned to stone for playing hurling on a Sunday.
- **The Cheesewring** — A natural granite formation that looks impossible (balanced stones). Folklore: a druid's cheese press.
- **Dozmary Pool** — Said to be where Excalibur was returned to the Lady of the Lake. Also claimed as bottomless.
- **Rough Tor and Brown Willy** — Highest points in Cornwall. Exposed, windswept, ancient.

**In the game:** Used for group trips (Ch5) and Maya path scenes. The moor provides the *wild* counterpoint to the coast — interior, raw, harder to love. "The coast is beautiful. The moor is honest." (Maya.)

## CORNISH LEGENDS AND DROLLS FOR THE GAME

### What is a Droll?
A *droll* is a Cornish folk tale, traditionally told by a *droll-teller* — a wandering storyteller who exchanged stories for food and shelter. Drolls are longer and more complex than fairy tales, often with moral ambiguity and dark endings. They encode local knowledge: which wells are safe, where the dangerous paths are, what to do when the sea turns.

### Drolls Used in the Game

#### 1. The Mermaid of Zennor
**Summary:** A beautiful woman appears at Zennor church every Sunday. She has the most beautiful voice in the congregation. A young man, Matthew Trewhella, falls in love with her. He follows her to the sea. Neither returns. Years later, a mermaid is seen off Pendour Cove. She asks the sailors to move their anchor — it's blocking the door to her house.

**Elena's version (Ch4 droll):**
> "Trewhella didn't drown, mind. He chose. She showed him where she lived and he looked at the church and he looked at the sea and he chose. The question is never 'was she real.' The question is 'what would you leave behind?'"

**Alchemical reading:** *Solutio* — dissolution in the unconscious. Trewhella is consumed by the anima. The mermaid is the depth that claims those who approach without preparation. The anchor blocking the door = the rational mind preventing access to the depths.

---

#### 2. The Pellar and the Ill-Wished Cow
**Summary:** A farmer's cow stops giving milk. He goes to the pellar, who tells him to take a bottle of the cow's urine, add nine pins, boil it at midnight, and wait. The person who ill-wished the cow will come to the door before dawn, in pain. The farmer must not let them in. At 2am, an old woman from the next village hammers on the door, screaming. The farmer holds firm. By morning, the cow gives milk again.

**Elena's version (wry):**
> "The point isn't whether you believe in the ill-wish. The point is that the cow stopped giving milk and the pellar solved the problem. You can call it psychology. The farmer called it the pellar's brass. Both were right."

**Amelia's journal note (if heard):** "Elena told me a story about a cow and some pins and I'm not sure if it was a parable or a recipe."

---

#### 3. The Knockers
**Summary:** Knockers are small spirits that live in the tin mines. They knock on the walls to guide miners to ore — or to warn them away from danger. You must leave them a portion of your *crowst* (packed lunch) or they'll lead you astray. Whistling underground angers them.

**In the game:** Referenced by Elena when talking about the fogou: *"The old miners left food for the knockers. They weren't superstitious — they were polite. You're about to enter someone else's house. Best remember your manners."*

---

#### 4. The Merry Maidens (Extended Droll)
**Tourist version:** Nineteen girls danced on a Sunday and were turned to stone as punishment. Two musicians (the Pipers) were similarly punished.

**Elena's version (older, darker):**
> "The girls knew what they were doing. The dance wasn't an accident — it was a working. Nineteen women in a circle on the night the worlds touch. The stones aren't a punishment. They're a *holding*. The dance is still happening. You just can't see it because you're thinking too fast."

---

#### 5. The Wild Hunt
**Cornish variant:** The *Dando and his Dogs* tradition — a priest called Dando hunts on Sundays, is taken by the Devil. In wider Cornish tradition, the Wild Hunt rides on stormy nights between autumn and spring endand woe to anyone caught in their path.

**In the game (Ch6, midwinter):** Elena or Maya references the Wild Hunt during the solstice scene: "On the longest night, the old stories say the hunt rides. Not to kill — to gather. Everything that's loose and lost and wandering. Best to be indoors, with a fire, and something to believe in."

## CORNISH NATURAL WORLD — SENSORY REFERENCE

### What Cornwall Looks, Sounds, Smells, and Feels Like

**Coast (Penwith):**
- **Visual:** Granite cliffs, turquoise water on sunny days, grey-green on overcast. Gorse (bright yellow, blooms almost year-round). Lichen-covered stone. Wild thyme. Heather (purple in late summer, bronze in winter).
- **Sound:** Seabirds (herring gulls, choughs, fulmars). Wind — always wind. Surf. The hollow sound of wind through caves.
- **Smell:** Salt. Gorse (smells like coconut). Wet earth. Seaweed at low tide.
- **Feel:** Wind pulling at clothes. Cold sharp air. Granite rough under fingers. Grass tussocks underfoot.

**Moor (Bodmin):**
- **Visual:** Rolling brown-green-grey. Tors breaking the skyline. Mist. Sheep. Stone walls. Pools of dark water.
- **Sound:** Wind. Always wind but different from the coast — lower, more constant. Ponies. Curlew cry (one of the loneliest sounds in Britain).
- **Smell:** Peat. Heather. Wet wool. Cold water.
- **Feel:** Boggier underfoot. Harsher wind. More exposed. You can see for miles but feel utterly alone.

**Villages (Penzance, Mousehole, Zennor):**
- **Visual:** Granite cottages. Narrow lanes. Fishing boats in harbour. Chapel Street's Georgian facades. Art galleries and charity shops. Blue pub signs.
- **Sound:** Gulls. Harbour water. Footsteps on granite sets. The tick of a pub clock.
- **Smell:** Fish and chips. Pub beer. Salt. Diesel from boats. Rain on stone.

**Interior Cornwall (between coast and moor):**
- **Visual:** Lanes so narrow they have passing places. Hedgerows with foxglove, campion, wild garlic. Farm gates. Church towers. Patchy mobile signal.
- **Sound:** Birdsong. Tractors. The silence between sounds (Cornwall's interior is genuinely quiet).
- **Smell:** Mud. Cow. Wild garlic in spring (overwhelming, sweet, everywhere). Cut grass.

## INTEGRATION GUIDELINES

### How to Use This Material

1. **Be specific.** Don't write "a stone circle in Cornwall." Write "the Merry Maidens, nineteen granite stones in a field of rough grass off the B3315, the sea visible in the gap between two hedgerows." Specificity is what makes it feel real.

2. **Weather matters.** Cornwall is rarely warm and never dry for long. Scenes set there should include weather as a character: "The rain came in sideways from the Atlantic, the kind that laughs at umbrellas."

3. **Don't romanticise.** Cornwall is not a fairy tale. It's a real place with real poverty, real unemployment, real struggles with depopulation and second-home ownership. Elena is passionate about this: "They buy the cottages and visit twice a year. The language dies because there's nobody left to speak it to."

4. **Respect the tradition.** The pellar tradition is treated as valid knowledge, not as quaint superstition. Elena is not a character for the player to be amused by. She is as rigorous and demanding as Hawthorne, in her own idiom.

5. **Kernewek is alive.** Use it in context, translated naturally or left untranslated when meaning is clear. It should feel like a living language, not a museum exhibit.
